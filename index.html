TASTE2: Text-Aligned Speech Modeling and Deployment toward Full-Duplex Voice Interaction

Abstract

Full-duplex voice interaction asks for more than turning one finished utterance into another. A system has to process speech as it arrives, judge when to take or yield the floor, and stop when the user interrupts, all without losing the linguistic competence it inherited from text pretraining or the acoustic cues that make a reply sound like an answer. Those goals usually pull against each other: giving speech its own tokens lengthens the sequence and dilutes what text pretraining taught the model. We ask whether TASTE (Text-Aligned Speech Tokenization and Embedding) offers a way out, and present TASTE2, which rebuilds the original utterance-level method into an incremental dialogue stack.

A shared text-token vocabulary removes word-level averaging across the Speech Tokenizer, Spoken LM, and Speech Detokenizer. Modality-aligned dialogue training predicts one continuous audio latent for each text token instead of interleaving heterogeneous token streams, and an incremental Speech Detokenizer enables streaming synthesis through CosyVoice2. The sequence therefore stays exactly as long as the text, and after speech and dialogue training TASTE2 (Merge) reaches 56.3% on LLaMA-Questions against a 57.3% Qwen2.5-7B Instruct text-only reference — a 98.2% accuracy retention, where interleaved systems in the same comparison retain between 29.7% and 83.1%.

We then take the model out of the benchmark harness. TASTE2 VoiceBot is a working research system that consumes user speech incrementally, streams synthesized audio, and halts generation the moment the user barges in. On Full-Duplex-Bench v1.0 it handles interruptions at 0.060 s while keeping its continuation coherent. To our knowledge we also give the first systematic characterization of explicit paralinguistic control in a TASTE-based model: speaking rate holds up under both training strategies after dialogue SFT, emotion control turns out to be strategy dependent, and the remaining attributes stay weak.

Method

The original TASTE aligns one speech representation with each text token, but it was built for complete utterances. Its Whisper-based tokenizer and LLaMA backbone disagree on vocabulary, so representations are averaged over word boundaries, and its synthesis stack waits for the whole utterance before producing audio. TASTE2 changes the vocabulary, the alignment, and the timing of synthesis so that one representation survives an incremental dialogue.

Architecture

Shared vocabulary

Speech Tokenizer, Spoken LM, and Speech Detokenizer all use the Spoken LM's tokenizer. Word-level averaging and its language-dependent segmentation disappear, and a standard chat template needs no conversion between token boundaries.

A frozen Distil-Whisper encoder feeds a decoder whose embedding layer is swapped for that vocabulary; its output goes to a Residual VQ instead of an LM head.

Aligned prediction

Each position sums a text-token embedding with its aligned audio latent rather than adding a sequence element. The audio target is shifted by one position, so the model commits to a word before deciding how to say it.

Backbone: Qwen2.5-7B with LoRA. The latent, not a sequence of quantized indices, is the regression target.

Streaming synthesis

The Speech Detokenizer maps text–latent pairs into CosyVoice2's S3 space in chunks — 15 S3 units for every 5 input pairs — so audio begins before the reply is complete.

Adapted from CosyVoice2's Qwen2.5-0.5B text-to-S3 model; the flow-matching model and vocoder downstream stay frozen.

Training

  1. Stage 1 — representation. The Speech Tokenizer and Speech Detokenizer are trained jointly, one learning to extract audio latents and the other to reconstruct audio from text-token and latent pairs. This stage fixes the latent space everything else depends on.
  2. Stage 2 — Spoken LM pretraining. A pretrained text LM is adapted with LoRA to predict aligned text–latent pairs over roughly 40,600 hours of speech from Emilia and LibriTTS. How well an acoustic attribute is represented here turns out to bound what any later fine-tuning can control.
  3. Stage SFT — dialogue. 143,507 dialogues (781,443 turns, 1,493 hours) teach response generation, turn boundaries, and interruption behavior. Natural corpora rarely mark interruptions, so 1,027 of those hours are synthesized specifically to contain barge-ins, disfluencies, and explicit style requests.

Two ways to get instruction following

Merge starts Stage 2 from Qwen2.5-7B and adds an instruction-following task vector by TIES merging before dialogue SFT, at no extra training cost. Direct simply starts from Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. Everything else is identical, which makes the pair a controlled comparison: the retention gap between them (98.2% against 92.4%) locates part of the loss in instruction tuning rather than in speech modeling.

Teaching a model to be interrupted

Turns are laid out in the ChatML template Qwen2.5 already uses, with audio attached to each text position instead of getting special tokens of its own. For an interrupted assistant turn, the loss weight on the closing <|im_end|> is set to zero — the turn ended, but not because the model chose to end it. The resulting <|im_end|> probability then serves directly as the turn-decision gate at deployment, with no separate classifier to train.

Results

The evaluations support a specific claim rather than a general one. Text-aligned tokenization keeps the text model's competence nearly intact and delivers the full-duplex behavior that depends most directly on that representation; latency and paralinguistic breadth are where it still falls short.

TASTE2 VoiceBot

Benchmarks measure a model; a conversation exercises a system. The VoiceBot puts TASTE2 behind a live audio loop: VAD opens the floor, the model's own distribution decides whether to take it, generated audio streams out as it is produced, and playback stops when the user speaks over it. Routing the turn decision through the model rather than a separate classifier is what makes the barge-in behavior possible, and also what costs the deployment part of its latency.

Paralinguistic Control

If an audio latent accompanies every text token, the model has somewhere to put acoustic intent — but only if training gives it something to put there. We test instruction-conditioned control on a 41-item subset of VStyle, scored 1–5 by three human raters. Speaking rate is the one attribute that holds under both training strategies after dialogue SFT; the two samples below are among its highest-rated generations.

Stage 2 pretraining behaves like a ceiling: an attribute with no solid representation there is not rescued by dialogue SFT, and one that succeeds there, such as whisper, may still not survive it. Emotion is the least settled case — angry and surprise clear the threshold under Direct and collapse under Merge.

Conclusion

TASTE2 establishes text-aligned speech modeling as a practical foundation for full-duplex voice interaction, and getting there required rebuilding the original utterance-level method end to end. Pairing one continuous audio latent with each text token keeps 98.2% of the text reference's accuracy while the model emits acoustic information at every step, and it delivers the behavior that depends most directly on that representation: barge-in at 0.060 s, and turn identification at 0.908 takeover rate with no dedicated classifier. The VoiceBot shows the same stack running as a live system.

What remains open is stated as plainly. Natural conversations are still harder than synthetic ones, deployed synthesis is slow enough to be felt, and paralinguistic control works for speaking rate while most other attributes do not follow. The evidence points to Stage 2 representation quality as the place to look next.
